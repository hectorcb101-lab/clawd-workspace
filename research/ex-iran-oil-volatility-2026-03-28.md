## Executive Summary

The 2026 Iran War, initiated on 28 February with Operation Epic Fury, has triggered the largest oil supply disruption in the history of the global energy market. The effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz — through which ~20% of the world's daily oil and LNG supply normally transits — has removed approximately 16 million barrels per day (Mbd) of petroleum products from global flows. Brent crude surged from ~$60/bbl pre-war to above $110/bbl by mid-March, recording the largest weekly gain in futures history (35% in a single week). The IEA has characterised this as the "greatest global energy and food security challenge in history." Emergency responses — including a historic 400-million-barrel strategic reserve release, OPEC+ production increases, and pipeline rerouting via Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast — have provided only partial relief against a disruption of this magnitude. The crisis continues to evolve, with analysts warning that oil could reach $150–200/bbl if the strait remains closed, and the OECD has already cut global growth forecasts while raising inflation projections.

## 1. The Strait of Hormuz: Scale of Disruption

### Pre-War Baseline
- **Normal daily transits:** ~120 vessels per day (both directions)
- **Oil flow:** ~20 million barrels per day (Mbd) of crude oil and condensates
- **LNG:** 20% of global LNG trade transited through Hormuz in 2024
- **Share of global supply:** ~20% of all seaborne oil; ~7.5% of total global supply

### Post-War Reality (as of late March 2026)
- **Transit collapse:** 94.2% reduction — from 120 daily transits to just 6.9
- **Physical vessel presence:** 84.4% reduction confirmed by satellite imagery (96 large vessels → 15)
- **Crude exports west of Hormuz:** Collapsed 87% — from 20.1 Mbd to 2.7 Mbd
- **Arabian Gulf port calls:** Down 47.3% in the first 14 days
- **Total petroleum disruption:** ~16 Mbd of petroleum products (crude, condensates, refined products, LPG, naphtha) stopped flowing

**The strait has not been formally "closed" in a traditional sense — it has shifted to a permission-based system.** A small number of vessels, primarily those with ties to Iran, transit through Iranian territorial waters rather than international shipping lanes. The vast majority of the global fleet has physically vacated the area.

### GPS Jamming
Iran has deployed persistent GPS jamming across the Gulf, affecting 1,100+ vessels on the first day of the conflict alone. AIS signals are being distorted, making maritime tracking unreliable without satellite imagery corroboration.

### Key Volatility Characteristics

1. **Speed of spike:** Brent rose faster in the first week than during any previous conflict-driven crisis — including the 1990 Gulf War, 2003 Iraq invasion, or 2022 Russia-Ukraine war.

2. **Structural vs. temporary:** Unlike previous spikes driven by fear/risk premium, this one reflects actual physical supply destruction. Paper/futures markets have lagged the physical market reality — physical crude delivered to China has already approached $100/bbl while futures initially stayed in the $80s.

3. **Product-specific divergence:** Jet fuel prices in Singapore surged ~140%. If crude had tracked jet fuel proportionally, Brent would be at ~$175/bbl. Diesel, LPG, and naphtha have seen similar disproportionate spikes.

4. **Concentration of conflict output:** The 11 countries currently engaged in major conflicts (Gulf + Ukraine) account for 51% of global crude production and 56% of global gas production — the highest such concentration since World War II.

## 3. Country-by-Country Supply Impact

### Iraq — Most Exposed
- Southern fields (85–90% of national output, 4+ Mbd) have no pipeline bypass
- Only ~40,000 bpd can be moved by truck
- Onshore storage hit 90% utilisation within 3 days of the closure
- Output at Rumaila and West Qurna 2 cut by 1.4–1.5 Mbd
- Near-total national power blackout from loss of associated gas and Iranian pipeline gas
- 1.5 Mbd of production shut down entirely

### Saudi Arabia — Partial Mitigation
- East-West Pipeline operating at maximum throughput (~5.5 Mbd to Yanbu on Red Sea)
- 57 VLCCs now routing to Yanbu; exports there at 12-month high (~5 Mbd)
- ~30 VLCCs anchored in Persian Gulf as floating storage
- **Limitation:** Can sustain current production for only ~20 days before storage exhaustion
- OPEC confirmed a "big Saudi production hike" on 11 March

### UAE — Under Fire
- ADNOC pipeline (1.8 Mbd capacity to Fujairah) intermittently operational
- Fujairah bunkering hub functionally offline after repeated drone strikes — bunker prices at historic highs, force majeure declared
- Port calls down significantly

### Kuwait — Storage Crisis
- Started cutting production after running out of storage space
- No bypass pipeline available
- Heavily dependent on Hormuz

### Qatar — LNG Catastrophe
- QatarEnergy declared force majeure on all LNG contracts
- Ras Laffan (world's largest LNG export hub) struck by Iran on 18 March — 17% LNG production capacity lost
- Damage will take 3–5 years to repair
- Full halt to gas production confirmed 19 March
- Asian LNG spot prices surged 140%+

### Iran — Resilient Through Adaptation
- Post-war crude exports to China: 18.18 million barrels (down from 56.33M pre-war, but far above other Gulf producers at near-zero)
- Kharg Island still operational despite US strikes — 18.36M barrels exported since 1 March
- Alternative route: Goreh-Jask pipeline + Kooh Mobarak terminal (bypasses Hormuz entirely)
- Permission-based transit system through Iranian territorial waters for allied vessels

## 4. Emergency Response Measures

### IEA Strategic Reserve Release
- **Scale:** 400 million barrels — the largest coordinated release in IEA history
- **Participants:** 32 IEA member nations
- **Context:** IEA nations hold 1.2 billion barrels public + 600 million barrels mandatory commercial = ~124 days of lost Gulf supply
- **Japan:** Committed 45 days' worth of reserves (15 private + 30 government)
- **US:** Historic SPR release announced; also loosened restrictions on Russian oil purchases and issued 60-day Jones Act waiver

### OPEC+ Response
- 1 March: OPEC+ agreed to a "modest" output boost — only 206,000 bpd
- 11 March: Saudi Arabia announced a larger production hike
- **Critical limitation:** Total OPEC spare capacity of ~4–5 Mbd cannot bridge a 16 Mbd gap

### Alternative Routes
| Route | Capacity | Status |
|-------|----------|--------|
| Saudi East-West Pipeline (Yanbu) | ~5.5 Mbd | Maximum throughput |
| ADNOC Pipeline (Fujairah) | 1.8 Mbd | Intermittent — port under attack |
| Cape of Good Hope rerouting | N/A — adds 2+ weeks transit | Active but vastly more expensive |
| Iran's Goreh-Jask Pipeline | ~1 Mbd | Operational (Iran only) |

**Combined bypass capacity: 7–8 Mbd — less than half of normal Hormuz flows.** No combination of bypass routes, strategic reserves, floating storage, and alternative suppliers can replace 16 Mbd on any practical timeline.

## 5. Macroeconomic & Global Impact

### Growth & Inflation
- **OECD (26 March):** Cut global GDP growth to 2.9% for 2026, projecting G20 inflation 1.2 percentage points higher
- **Oxford Economics:** Revised baseline assumes prolonged conflict; broad-based forecast downgrades
- **ECB:** Postponed planned interest rate cuts on 19 March; raised 2026 inflation forecast, cut GDP growth projections
- **UK inflation:** Expected to breach 5% in 2026
- **Guardian/economists:** Warning of stagflation if oil sustains above $120–140/bbl
- **Goldman Sachs, IMF, CFR:** All issued significant downgrade reports

### Sector-Specific Impacts

**Refining:** Asia faces ~3 Mbd reduction in throughput. South Korea (highest exposure), Thailand, and India most affected. China has ordered near-total halt to March product exports. India considering withholding clean product exports.

**Aviation:** Airlines face kerosene/jet fuel prices that have more than doubled. Emirates, Etihad, Qatar Airways suspended all operations. Airspace closures across Bahrain, Iraq, Israel, Kuwait, Qatar, Syria, UAE. Thousands stranded globally — largest repatriation since COVID-19.

**Food security:** GCC states rely on the strait for 80%+ of caloric intake. 70% of food imports disrupted by mid-March. Consumer prices spiked 40–120% across the region. Iranian strikes on desalination plants threaten 99% of drinking water supply in Kuwait and Qatar.

**European energy:** Gas storage at ~30% capacity (historically low after harsh winter). Dutch TTF gas benchmarks nearly doubled to €60+/MWh. Shell warned Europe could face fuel shortage by April. Industrial surcharges of up to 30% imposed by chemical and steel manufacturers.

**Sanctions erosion:** EU enforcement of sanctions on Russian-derived products is softening. Cargoes of potentially Russian-origin diesel clearing in European ports. Probability of Russian energy re-entering European supply chains estimated at ~30% (up from negligible).

### Consumer Impact
- US gasoline: Up ~27 cents/gallon in one week (to $3.25 as of early March; likely higher now)
- European petrol: Visible spike (documented 30%+ in Germany within one week)
- Vietnam, Thailand: Fuel shortages and panic buying
- Global airfares: Rising sharply as airlines pass through jet fuel costs

## 6. Forward-Looking Analysis

### Price Scenarios

| Scenario | Brent Forecast | Probability |
|----------|---------------|-------------|
| Quick resolution (strait reopens within weeks) | $85–95/bbl settling | Low (~15%) |
| Prolonged closure (months) | $120–150/bbl sustained | Moderate (~50%) |
| Escalation (port attacks, production destruction) | $150–200/bbl | Moderate (~35%) |

Qatar's energy minister warned crude could reach $150/bbl "in coming weeks." Iran's military spokesperson threatened $200/bbl. Multiple investment banks have revised forecasts sharply upward — Macquarie, JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs all hiking estimates.

### Structural Risks
1. **Physical market divergence:** Futures markets have consistently underpriced the physical reality. When this gap closes, the adjustment will be sharp.
2. **Storage exhaustion:** Saudi Arabia and UAE have ~20 days before they must cut production regardless of pipeline capacity.
3. **Refinery cascade:** Asian run cuts will create product shortages (jet fuel, diesel, gasoline) that compound the crude supply shock.
4. **LNG structural damage:** Qatar's Ras Laffan damage (3–5 year repair) means LNG tightness persists long after any ceasefire.
5. **Insurance and shipping:** War risk premiums, rerouting costs, and force majeure declarations have fundamentally altered the economics of Gulf shipping.
6. **Sanction erosion:** The crisis is actively undermining the Western sanctions architecture against Russia.

### What to Watch
- Whether Iran expands attacks to Saudi/UAE export infrastructure on the Red Sea
- Saudi Arabia's storage utilisation timeline (~20 days from mid-March)
- IEA reserve release pace and coordination effectiveness
- Ceasefire negotiations and any credible de-escalation signals
- China's response to LNG supply crisis and potential coal substitution at scale
- European gas storage trajectory heading into summer refill season
